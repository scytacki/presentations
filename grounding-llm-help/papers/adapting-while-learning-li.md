# Adapting While Learning — teaching an LLM *when* to call the solver, not just how

**Paper:** *Adapting While Learning: Grounding LLMs for Scientific Problems with Intelligent
Tool Usage Adaptation*
**Authors:** Bohan Lyu, Yadi Cao, Duncan Watson-Parris, Leon Bergen, Taylor Berg-Kirkpatrick,
Rose Yu
**Venue:** arXiv:2411.00412 (cs.CL / cs.AI), submitted Nov 2024; subsequently ICML 2025 ·
https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00412

This is the strongest evidence in the scan for the "connect the LLM to the real model" strategy:
it shows, with numbers, that letting an LLM call external tools (numerical solvers, a physics
engine, and neural-surrogate simulators — see below) both **raises scientific accuracy** and
**cuts hallucination**. But the interesting part for us is not that
result — it's the *nuance the paper had to add to make it work*. Naively fine-tuning a model to
use tools makes it lean on them for everything, which is wasteful and, in a tutoring setting,
pedagogically wrong. The contribution is teaching the model **when** to reach for the tool versus
when to just reason. (Note up front: the domain here is scientific problem-solving over
benchmarks, not a student-facing K-12 simulation. The mechanism transfers; the setting does not.)

## The problem it addresses

LLMs **hallucinate when solving scientific problems** — they confidently produce wrong mechanisms,
equations, and numbers. Connecting the model to **external tools** (solvers, simulators) is a known
fix: ground the answer in something that actually computes. But the authors flag a second-order
failure that the naive fix introduces: **"models fine-tuned on tool usage become overreliant on
them and incur unnecessary costs."** Once you train a model to call the tool, it calls the tool for
*everything*, including problems it could have reasoned through directly. So the real problem is
two-sided: get the grounding benefit of tools **without** turning the model into something that can
no longer think on its own and burns compute on trivial cases.

## What it actually does (step by step)

The method, **AWL (Adapting While Learning)**, is a two-stage post-training recipe:

1. **World Knowledge Learning (WKL).** The model first **internalizes scientific knowledge by
   learning from solutions generated by the tools/simulators themselves.** Rather than just being
   taught "call this API," it is trained on the tool-produced solutions so the knowledge becomes
   part of the model — it learns *the science*, using the tool as the source of ground truth.
2. **Tool Usage Adaptation (TUA).** The model is then taught **when** to use a tool. Problems are
   **classified as easy or hard based on the model's measured accuracy** on them: if the model
   already answers a class of problem reliably, it's "easy"; if it doesn't, it's "hard." The model
   is then trained to **reason directly on easy problems** and **switch to the computational tool
   on hard ones.**

So "adapting while learning" is exactly that pairing: *learn* the world knowledge from the tool
(WKL), then *adapt* tool use to difficulty (TUA). The grounding is selective by design — the tool
is a fallback for the cases the model can't be trusted on, not a crutch for all of them.

## What it ran on

- **Six scientific benchmark datasets.** Two are existing public benchmarks — **MATH** and
  **SciBench** (numerical-answer science/math problems). Four are **custom datasets the authors
  built themselves**: **Mujoco** (rigid- and soft-body dynamics), **PDEs** (1D/2D partial
  differential equations for heat transfer and population dynamics), **Climate Science**, and
  **Epidemiology**. The four custom sets are multiple-choice (MCQ) format (plus some open-ended
  policy questions in Climate/Epidemiology); the two public sets are numerical-answer.
- **What the "tools" actually are — and it varies by dataset.** This matters for us, so be precise:
  - *Mujoco* — scenarios (e.g. a double pendulum, friction tests) run on the **MuJoCo physics
    engine**, wrapped as APIs. This is a real physics simulator.
  - *PDEs* — **in-house numerical solvers** for the heat-transfer / population-dynamics equations.
  - *Climate Science* and *Epidemiology* — APIs wrapping **pre-trained neural-network surrogate
    models (emulators)** of those dynamics (climate emulator adapted from MFRNP, epidemiology from
    INP). These are *approximations* of a simulation, not the full simulator — worth flagging.
  - *MATH* and *SciBench* — the model writes scripts against **SymPy / NumPy** library APIs.
  So "the tool" ranges from a genuine physics engine to learned emulators to symbolic-math
  libraries. None of them is a live simulation a student is steering; all are back-end compute the
  model calls and gets an answer from.
- The base model is **Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct**, post-trained with AWL. (A 14B Qwen2.5 variant is
  also tried in a scalability check.)
- Compared against strong general models including **GPT-4o** and **Claude-3.5-Sonnet**
  (also Llama-3.1-70B, GPT-4o-mini).

## How it was evaluated, and what they found

The headline results, averaged across the six benchmarks and measured **against the same 8B base
instruct model** (this is the comparison the percentages are drawn against — not against GPT-4o):

- **+29.11% answer accuracy** for the AWL-trained 8B model over the base 8B model.
- **+12.72% tool-usage accuracy** — i.e. the model gets *better at choosing when the tool is the
  right move*, not just at calling it. (The abstract's word is "accuracy," not "precision"; in
  Table 2 most other models hover near 50% — chance — on this decision, while AWL reaches ~64%.)

(The current arXiv abstract reports these as +29.11% / +12.72%; an earlier version cited during the
research scan gave +28.18% / +13.89%. The difference is immaterial to the story.)

The "small 8B model beats the frontier" claim is real but **narrower than it sounds, so state it
precisely**: the AWL-trained 8B surpasses GPT-4o and Claude-3.5-Sonnet **only on the four
custom-built datasets** — and there the margins are large, driven mostly by Climate and
Epidemiology, where the frontier models score in the 30s% (Climate: AWL 83.3 vs GPT-4o 35.8 /
Claude 32.5; Epidemiology: AWL 74.4 vs 32.5 / 35.0). On the **two public benchmarks it does not
win**: GPT-4o beats it on MATH (82.9 vs 62.4) and SciBench (71.7 vs 34.2). So the headline is a
fair *but selective* framing — it holds on the authors' own datasets, which are exactly the
problems their tools and training pipeline were constructed around, and where frontier models have
no tool access and little prior exposure. That is a real result for "grounding beats a bigger
ungrounded model on the grounded domain," but it is not "8B beats GPT-4o in general."

## The key insight: tools help — but the model must learn *when*, not just *how*

This is the part worth carrying forward. The naive lesson from "tools reduce hallucination" is
"always call the tool." The paper's finding is that this backfires: a model fine-tuned to use tools
becomes **overreliant**, calling the solver even for problems it could answer directly, wasting
compute and abandoning its own reasoning. The fix — **classify by difficulty (measured accuracy)
and reason directly on easy problems, tool only on hard ones** — is what makes grounding actually
work in practice. The interesting variable isn't *whether* to ground but *how to gate* the
grounding. For a tutor, that gate is also a pedagogical one: you don't want the system outsourcing
every question to a simulator when a direct, explainable answer would serve the student better.

## Why it matters for us

- This is the **strongest quantitative evidence for [the content anchor](../grounding-literature-review.md)** —
  grounding the LLM to the simulation's actual model/state. It shows that routing hard scientific
  questions through a real computational tool **materially improves accuracy and reduces
  hallucination**, with concrete numbers behind it. It is the technique that would stop an LLM
  from **confabulating physics** rather than reporting what the simulation actually computes.
- It directly addresses **[Scenario 2 — invented mechanism](../response-types-and-failures.md)**.
  An LLM left to reason unaided will invent a plausible-but-wrong mechanism; grounding the hard
  cases in the simulator's own computation is the structural defense against exactly that failure.
- The **easy/hard gating is a feature we'd want, not just a cost optimization.** It says: don't
  send everything to the model. For a student-facing tutor, knowing *when* to defer to the
  simulation versus when to explain directly is part of good pedagogy, and this paper gives a
  principled way to draw that line (by measured reliability).
- **One genuine point of contact with our simulation interest:** two of the four custom tools (Climate,
  Epidemiology) are **neural-surrogate emulators** of a simulation — the LLM is grounded against a
  model of the dynamics, which is conceptually close to grounding a tutor against a running sim. The
  others are a physics engine (Mujoco) and numerical/symbolic solvers. So the paper does show
  grounding against simulation-like back ends, not only against closed-form answer keys.
- **Caveat on transfer:** the setting is scientific problem-solving over benchmarks, not a
  student interacting with a K-12 sim. The "tool" returns a correct answer the model reports; it is
  not a live simulation a student is manipulating, and there is no student in the loop. The grounding
  *mechanism* is the transferable result; the evaluation does not tell us how this plays out in a
  tutoring dialogue. The entire "tutoring / pedagogy / student" framing in this write-up is **our
  extrapolation** — the paper makes no student-facing or pedagogical claim.

## Limitations (stated and evident)

- The paper **does not foreground an explicit limitations list** in the abstract; the ones below
  are evident rather than claimed.
- **Four of the six datasets are author-built, and the strongest results are on exactly those.**
  The custom datasets, their tools, and the training pipeline were all constructed together, so the
  headline "beats GPT-4o" win is on home turf — there's an inherent risk the benchmarks are shaped
  to fit the method. On the two independent public benchmarks (MATH, SciBench) the 8B model does not
  beat the frontier models. This doesn't invalidate the grounding result, but it should temper how
  much weight the "small model beats GPT-4o" line carries.
- **Domain mismatch with our setting:** benchmarks of scientific problems, not student-facing,
  interactive simulations. No student-interaction, dialogue-quality, or misconception-handling
  results.
- The **easy/hard classification depends on measured model accuracy**, which means the gating is
  only as good as the difficulty estimate — a model wrong-but-confident on a class of problems
  could be mis-gated to "reason directly" exactly where it should have called the tool.
- Results are tied to a specific **8B base model** and the authors' tool/simulator setup;
  the absolute figures are properties of that configuration.
- "Tool usage precision" measures *choosing the tool well*, not whether the resulting student-facing
  explanation is correct, clear, or pedagogically appropriate — that gap is left open.
