# World Simulator — can an LLM predict what a simulation does on its own?

**Paper:** *Can Language Models Serve as Text-Based World Simulators?*
**Authors:** Ruoyao Wang, Graham Todd, Ziang Xiao, Xingdi Yuan, Marc-Alexandre Côté, Peter Clark,
Peter Jansen
**Venue:** ACL 2024 (short paper) · arXiv:2406.06485 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06485 ·
https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-short.1/

This is the paper that gives us the empirical backbone for a claim we keep making: an LLM asked to
*be* the simulation — to compute, from its own knowledge, what the model would do next — gets it
wrong often enough that you cannot trust it. It is the research behind
[Scenario 2 (the confabulated mechanism)](../response-types-and-failures.md) in document 1. It is
worth reading carefully, and worth being honest about the domain gap, because the experiment is on
*text games*, not on continuous-physics PhET-style sims. The evidence transfers to our setting **by
analogy**, not directly.

## The problem it addresses

A "world simulator" is anything that, given a current state and an action, tells you the next state.
The paper asks a clean question: **can a large language model play that role directly?** If it
could, you could skip building and running an explicit simulation engine and just let the LLM
predict each step. That is exactly the tempting shortcut in our setting — let the LLM tell the
student what the simulation "would" show, rather than reading the answer off the running model. The
paper sets out to measure how reliable that shortcut actually is.

## What it actually does / the benchmark

The authors introduce **ByteSized32-State-Prediction (ByteSized32-SP)**, a benchmark built from
**31 distinct text games**, each derived from the human-authored ByteSized32 game set in which every
game **simulates a different scientific or common-sense reasoning concept** (the running example in
the paper is *boil water*). Each game's state is expressed as structured JSON — objects with
**properties** (e.g., temperature, size) and **relationships** to other objects — which lets the
authors probe transitions precisely rather than just scoring free text.

They formalize the task as **LLM-Sim**: given the current game state, an action, and the game's
rules, predict the next state. Crucially they split transitions along two axes:

1. **Action-driven vs. environment-driven.** Action-driven transitions are the direct result of what
   the agent just did; environment-driven transitions are what the world's own *dynamics* do on the
   next tick (the part where the underlying engine's rules — heat transfer, the like — actually
   run).
2. **Dynamic vs. static.** A transition is *dynamic* if a non-trivial property actually changes;
   *static* if nothing meaningfully changes. The interesting science lives in the dynamic
   transitions.

So the benchmark isolates the hard case: can the LLM reproduce the **environment's underlying
dynamics** — the arithmetic, common-sense, and scientific reasoning the game engine encodes — rather
than just echoing an unchanged state.

## What it ran on

- **GPT-4** is the headline model, evaluated with in-context learning. **GPT-3.5 was also tested**
  (Appendix E) and did substantially worse — so the paper actually reports *two* models, not one,
  and the GPT-4 numbers are the better of the two.
- The model is run under **three rule conditions**: game rules written by a **human expert**, rules
  **generated by an LLM** (GPT-4 reading the game code), and **no rules at all**. The headline best
  numbers come from the rules-provided conditions; removing the rules makes performance worse (game-
  progress accuracy, for instance, drops from 92.1% to 61.5%). So the rules-in-context setting is the
  model's *best* case, not a handicap.
- **31 text games** from ByteSized32, each a small simulator of one scientific or common-sense
  concept.
- They also run a **preliminary human study** (4 annotators — the paper's authors — on 5 games)
  comparing humans against GPT-4. See the numbers below; note the subset was deliberately sampled to
  pin GPT-4 near 50%, so the human-vs-model gap is illustrative, not a calibrated head-to-head.

## How it was evaluated, and what they found

The metric is **whole-transition prediction accuracy against the game engine's ground-truth next
state** — a transition counts as correct only if the model reproduces the *entire* predicted state
(or state-diff) the real simulator would produce; one wrong property fails the transition. Accuracy
is then averaged per game. This is an exact-match-of-the-full-state criterion, not partial credit,
so the failure rates below are "got the whole next state right" rates.

The headline numbers (best across rule conditions; on the *dynamic* slice where a non-trivial change
actually occurs):

- On **full state transitions**, model accuracy **does not exceed 59.9%**. Roughly **40% of those
  transitions are wrong**.
- On the hard part — **dynamic environment-driven transitions** (the world's own dynamics producing
  a real change) — GPT-4 is correct on **at most 49.7%**. Call it a coin flip on exactly the
  transitions where the science happens.
- By contrast, on **dynamic action-driven transitions** (direct consequences of the agent's action)
  GPT-4 does much better, **up to 77.1%**.

The human study makes the gap concrete: on a subset deliberately sampled to put GPT-4 around 50%,
**human annotators averaged ~80%** while **GPT-4 averaged ~50%** (per-game accuracy modeling the full
transition F). The paper's read: the task is "generally straightforward and relatively easy for
humans," leaving "significant room for improvement for LLMs." Because the subset was normalized to
GPT-4's 50%, treat this as an existence proof that humans clear the bar the model misses, not as a
clean apples-to-apples score.

The paper's own gloss: **"the most challenging part of the LLM-Sim task is likely simulating the
underlying environmental dynamics."** Static transitions (nothing really changes) are
"substantially easier" than dynamic ones across most conditions — which is to say the model is
fine when the right answer is "not much happened" and falls apart when the answer requires actually
computing a change.

And the failures are not random noise. The paper's per-property breakdown (Figure 2) shows they
cluster **precisely on the non-trivial properties that require arithmetic, common-sense, or
scientific reasoning** — the paper names quantitative ones like `temperature` and
`timeAboveMaxTemp`, and notes a common failure is leaving a value *unaltered* that should have
changed. These are the quantitative, dynamics-driven properties. The model is weakest exactly where
a science simulation is most valuable.

And the single-step number understates the practical problem because errors compound. The paper
notes that at ~59.9% per-step accuracy, **after 10 steps simulation accuracy would fall to
0.599^10 — under 1%.** A simulator that is wrong ~40% of the time per step is effectively useless
over any real trajectory.

The paper's conclusion, stated plainly: despite GPT-4's impressive performance, **"it is still an
unreliable world simulator without further innovations."**

## The one thing to keep straight: text games are not continuous-physics sims

**ByteSized32 games are discrete, text/JSON-state simulators**, not the continuous-variable,
real-time PhET-style simulations our students use. Boiling water as a text-game state transition is
not the same computational object as a PhET energy-or-temperature sim with sliders and live plots.

That matters for how we cite this. The experiment does **not** directly measure LLM reliability on
continuous physics. What it establishes — robustly, with numbers — is that **even in a simplified,
fully-specified, discrete world where the rules are handed to the model in-context, GPT-4 cannot
reliably reproduce the dynamics.** If it can't do it there, the case that it would do it for a
richer continuous sim is weaker, not stronger. So this is **evidence by analogy**: it supports our
conclusion for the PhET setting rather than proving it.

## Why it matters for us

- It is the **direct empirical support for [the content anchor](../grounding-literature-review.md)** — grounding
  to the simulation's actual model and state rather than to the LLM's guess about the model. The
  ~40% full-transition error rate is the concrete reason "let the LLM compute what the sim does" is
  not a safe design.
- It is the **research behind [Scenario 2 (the confabulated mechanism)](../response-types-and-failures.md)**
  in document 1: an LLM that narrates what the simulation "would" do is doing exactly the LLM-Sim
  task this paper shows it fails ~40% of the time — and fails *worst* on the temperature-style
  scientific properties a student is most likely to ask about.
- The design takeaway is sharp: **don't let the LLM be the simulator.** Connect it to the real
  executed model — read state out of the running sim, feed actions into the running sim — and let
  the LLM do language, framing, and pedagogy on top of state it did not have to compute.

## Limitations (stated and evident)

- **Domain gap, again.** Text-game state prediction is not continuous-physics simulation. The
  numbers are real but transfer to our setting by analogy, not by direct measurement.
- **Two models, one era.** The headline results are **GPT-4**, with **GPT-3.5** also tested (it did
  markedly worse). Both are 2024-vintage frontier-ish models; newer models may close some of the gap,
  and the paper explicitly frames the benchmark as a way to "track future progress as new models
  appear." The *direction* (dynamics are the hard part; quantitative/scientific properties fail most)
  is the durable finding; the exact percentages are a snapshot. No fine-tuned or specialized
  simulator models were evaluated — only off-the-shelf in-context learning.
- **Best-case framing.** The headline numbers come from the conditions where the model was *given the
  game rules in-context* (human-written or LLM-generated); a no-rules condition was also run and did
  worse. In our setting an LLM usually is **not** handed the sim's exact governing equations, so the
  rules-provided numbers are closer to an upper bound on LLM-as-simulator reliability than a typical-
  case estimate.
- **Discrete, small worlds.** The games are small, single-concept simulators; this says nothing
  about scaling to complex, multi-variable simulations beyond "the hard part only gets harder."
